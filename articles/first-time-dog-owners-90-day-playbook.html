The First-Time Dog Owner's 90-Day Playbook: Training, Gear, Vet, and Budget

By The First 90 Days Team · Published September 27, 2026 · 14 min read

This article contains affiliate links. If you buy through them, we may earn a commission at no extra cost to you. Read our full disclosure.

You just got a dog. Congratulations — and welcome to the most exhausting, wonderful three months of your life. If you're lying awake wondering whether the crying in the crate is normal, whether you bought the right food, or how much this is all going to cost, this guide is for you.

Here's the truth nobody tells first-time owners: the first 90 days are a project, not a vibe. You don't need to be a natural dog person. You need a plan. This playbook gives you one — week by week, with the training priorities, gear, vet visits, and budget for each phase.

A quick note: This is general information from experienced owners, not veterinary advice. Your vet knows your specific dog. For any health or medical decision — vaccines, spaying/neutering, illness — ask your vet.

Why the first 90 days matter so much

Puppies have a socialization window that closes around 14–16 weeks. Adult rescues form their attachment patterns in the first 6–8 weeks in a new home. Training habits — good and bad — calcify fast: a puppy that learns jumping gets attention will still be jumping at 60 pounds.

The good news: you don't need to be perfect. You need to be consistent for about three months. Dogs are forgiving learners; they're not forgiving of chaos with no pattern. Give them a pattern, and the rest gets dramatically easier.

Here's how the 90 days break down:

  • Days 1–14: Survival mode. Bonding, potty routine, crate introduction, first vet visit.
  • Days 15–42: Foundations. House training rhythm, basic commands, socialization starts.
  • Days 43–90: Real life. Leash skills, longer alone time, vet follow-ups, settling into your actual routine.

Phase 1: Days 1–14 — Survive and bond

Your only training goals: name, crate, and potty

Forget "sit" for now. In the first two weeks, your dog needs to learn three things: their name means good things, the crate is safe, and outside is where the bathroom is.

The potty schedule (this is the whole game): Take the dog out every 2 hours during the day, plus immediately after waking up, after eating, after drinking, and after playing. For an 8-week-old puppy, that's roughly 10–12 trips a day. Yes, really. Set a timer. Puppies physically cannot hold it long — the rule of thumb is one hour per month of age, so an 8-week-old gets about 2 hours. For the complete hour-by-hour version, follow our potty training schedule for weeks 8–12.

Crate introduction: Feed every meal in the crate with the door open. Toss treats in randomly during the day. First closed-door sessions should be 5 minutes while you're in the room, building up gradually. A crying puppy at night is normal for the first 3–5 nights — make sure they've pottied, then keep nighttime interactions boring and dark. For exact day-by-day timings, use our 14-day crate training method.

Gear you need before (or on) day one

Buy these before the dog arrives. Everything else can wait (the full list, with what to look for in each item, is in our new dog essentials checklist):

  • Crate sized for the dog's adult weight, with a divider panel for puppies (see our breed-by-breed crate sizing chart)
  • 6-foot leash (not retractable) and a properly fitted collar or harness
  • Food and water bowls — stainless steel, skip the cute ceramic for now
  • The same food the dog is currently eating (breeder/shelter food), enough for 2 weeks — switch foods gradually later to avoid stomach upset
  • Training treats — small, soft, smelly; you'll use hundreds in month one
  • 2–3 chew toys (different textures) and one puzzle feeder
  • Enzyme cleaner for accidents — regular cleaner doesn't remove the scent marker, and the dog will go there again
  • Poop bags — buy the big box now; you'll use 3–5 a day

Vet: book the first visit within 72 hours

Even if the breeder or shelter says the dog "just saw a vet," book your own visit within the first 3 days. The first appointment covers: a full physical exam, weight check, fecal test for parasites, vaccine review (bring any records you were given), and a microchip check. Ask about the vaccine schedule going forward, flea/tick/heartworm prevention for your area, and what number to call after hours.

Budget: expect the biggest hit now

Month one is the most expensive month of dog ownership. Between the adoption fee or breeder cost, the gear haul, the first vet visit, and pet insurance signup, most first-time owners spend $800–$2,000 in the first 30 days before food is even a factor. That's normal. It drops sharply after. For the full picture, see our real first-year cost breakdown with three worked example budgets.

Phase 2: Days 15–42 — Build the foundations

House training: keep the schedule, add the bell (optional)

By week 3, you should see fewer accidents — not zero, fewer. Keep the every-2-hours schedule but start stretching it by 15 minutes each week the dog stays accident-free. The goal by day 42: the puppy signals at the door or holds it 3–4 hours during the day. Nighttime: most puppies sleep through (6–7 hours) by 12–14 weeks.

The accident rule: If you catch them mid-accident, interrupt calmly and take them outside immediately. If you find it after the fact, clean it with enzyme cleaner and say nothing — punishment after the fact teaches the dog to hide it from you, not to hold it.

The five commands that matter (in this order)

  1. Name recognition — say the name, treat when they look. 20 reps a day.
  2. Sit — lure with a treat over the nose. The foundation of impulse control.
  3. Come (recall) — practice indoors first, always reward, never punish a slow recall.
  4. Down — useful for vet visits and settling.
  5. Leave it — the command that keeps them from eating garbage on walks.

Train in 5-minute sessions, 2–3 times a day. Puppies check out after 5 minutes; short sessions beat long ones every time. End every session with a win.

Socialization: the clock is ticking

The socialization window closes around 14–16 weeks, and you can't get it back. This doesn't mean dog parks (avoid those until vaccines are complete — ask your vet). It means exposure: 100 different positive experiences. Car rides. Umbrellas. Men with hats. Kids on scooters. Vacuum cleaners. Different floor textures. Elevators. Each exposure should be paired with treats and kept short — the goal is "that was fine," not "that was intense."

Keep a simple checklist on your phone and aim for 3–5 new experiences a week.

Vet: vaccines continue on schedule

Puppies typically get a series of vaccinations every 3–4 weeks until around 16 weeks (your vet sets the exact schedule). Bring your vaccine records to every visit. This is also when to discuss spay/neuter timing — there's no single right answer; it depends on breed, size, and sex, and it's a conversation to have with your vet, not a blog post.

Alone-time training starts now

Separation anxiety is easier to prevent than to fix. Starting in week 3: leave the dog alone in their crate or pen for 5 minutes while you're home, then 15, then 30. Vary it. Don't make departures emotional — boring exits, boring returns. If you work from home, practice this deliberately; dogs that never experience alone time until you return to the office have the hardest adjustment.

Phase 3: Days 43–90 — Real life begins

Leash training: the long game

Loose-leash walking takes months, not weeks — set expectations now. The method: the second the leash goes tight, stop walking. When the dog checks in or the leash slackens, move forward and reward. Every walk is training; there are no "just walks" in the first 90 days. A front-clip harness helps manage pulling while you train (it doesn't train the dog for you — nothing does).

Longer alone time and the real routine

By day 90, most dogs should handle 3–4 hours alone comfortably; adult dogs longer. This is when your actual life schedule locks in: morning walk, work block, midday break, evening walk, training session. Dogs thrive on predictable routines — the same order of events matters more than the same clock time.

The adolescent preview

Around 4–6 months, many puppies hit a regression: commands they "knew" evaporate, chewing intensifies, energy spikes. This is normal adolescence, not failure. Go back to basics, increase exercise (sniff walks tire a dog out more than fetch), and don't panic. Almost every owner thinks they've broken their dog at month 4. You haven't.

Vet: the 90-day check-in

By day 90 your dog should have: completed the initial vaccine series (per your vet's schedule), started flea/tick/heartworm prevention, and had the spay/neuter conversation. Keep a simple health log — dates of vaccines, weight at each visit, any reactions. Future-you at the emergency vet at midnight will be grateful.

The essential gear checklist (recap)

Must-have before day one: crate with divider, 6-foot leash, collar/harness, stainless bowls, 2 weeks of current food, training treats, 2–3 chew toys, puzzle feeder, enzyme cleaner, poop bags.

Buy in month 1–2: dog bed (after chewing phase eases — many owners waste a nice bed in week 2), playpen or baby gates, grooming basics (nail clippers, brush suited to coat type), car restraint or barrier.

Skip for now: expensive orthopedic beds, retractable leashes (they teach pulling), clothing unless your breed genuinely needs it, bulk food subscriptions until you know what agrees with their stomach.

The emergency fund rule

The rule: Before anything else, set aside $1,000 in a dedicated pet emergency fund — or get insurance in week 1. One swallowed sock, one broken tooth, one midnight ER visit runs $800–$3,000. The owners who get blindsided aren't the ones who budgeted wrong; they're the ones who didn't budget for the surprise at all.

The 90-day checklist (print this)

  • Days 1–3: Vet visit booked and completed; vaccine records filed
  • Days 1–7: Potty schedule running (every 2 hours); crate introduced positively
  • Week 2: Pet insurance decided (bought or consciously declined); microchip registered
  • Week 3: Alone-time training started; first 10 socialization experiences logged
  • Week 4: Name, sit, and come in progress; accident frequency dropping
  • Week 6: Vaccine series on track; 40+ socialization experiences logged
  • Week 8: Walking on leash without constant pulling (imperfect is fine)
  • Week 10: 3–4 hours alone without distress; spay/neuter timing discussed with vet
  • Day 90: Routine locked in; emergency fund funded; you actually enjoy your dog

FAQ

Is it normal for my puppy to cry in the crate at night?
Yes, for the first 3–5 nights. Make sure they've pottied, keep it dark and boring, and don't turn nighttime wake-ups into playtime. If crying persists past a week or is paired with drooling, escape attempts, or panic, talk to your vet or a trainer — that's beyond normal adjustment.
How long does potty training take?
Most puppies are reliably house-trained by 4–6 months with a consistent schedule. "Reliable" means occasional accidents, not zero — full bladder control develops gradually. If you're still seeing multiple daily accidents at 6 months, check with your vet to rule out medical causes.
When should I start training classes?
Puppy kindergarten-style classes are typically open once vaccines are underway (often around 10–12 weeks — confirm with your vet). The value isn't the commands; it's supervised socialization with other puppies. For adult rescues, a basic manners class in month 2 is ideal.
Should I get pet insurance?
If a surprise $2,000 vet bill would wreck your finances, yes — and sign up in the first weeks, because pre-existing conditions generally aren't covered and waiting periods apply. Compare accident-only vs. accident-and-illness plans, deductibles, and reimbursement rates — not just the monthly premium. Our pet insurance explainer walks through a real claim line by line.
Can I leave my puppy alone while I work?
An 8-week-old puppy maxes out around 2 hours; build up gradually to 4 hours by 4–6 months. For full workdays before that, you'll need a midday dog walker, daycare a few days a week, or a work-from-home arrangement. This is the logistics problem most new owners underestimate — solve it before day one.

Remember: this guide covers general information, not medical advice. Vaccines, spay/neuter timing, illness, and behavior concerns all deserve a conversation with your veterinarian, who knows your individual dog. You've got this — and so does your dog.
