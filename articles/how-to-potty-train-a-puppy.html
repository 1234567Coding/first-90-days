How to Potty Train a Puppy: An Hour-by-Hour Schedule for Weeks 8–12

By The First 90 Days Team · Published September 27, 2026 · 9 min read

This article contains affiliate links. If you buy through them, we may earn a commission at no extra cost to you. Read our full disclosure.

Your 9-week-old puppy just peed on the rug twelve minutes after peeing outside, and you're starting to suspect they're doing it on purpose. They're not. At 8 to 12 weeks, a puppy physically can't hold it for long — what they can learn is a pattern, and that pattern is 100% your job. Here it is, hour by hour.

The one rule that governs everything: bladder math

There's a widely used guideline, repeated by the AKC and veterinary behaviorists: a puppy can hold their bladder roughly one hour for every month of age. An 8-week-old can hold it about 2 hours; a 12-week-old, about 3. Individual puppies vary — treat it as a ceiling, not a target.

Do the math and the mystery disappears. Your puppy isn't "being bad" at 90 minutes since the last trip — they're out of capacity. Most of what looks like a training problem at this age is a scheduling problem.

The 7 triggers: when you go out no matter what

Between the scheduled trips, puppies need to go after predictable events. Memorize this list — it covers most accidents:

  1. First thing in the morning. Don't check your phone. Shoes on, puppy up, straight outside. A full bladder after a night's sleep is the most urgent trip of the day.
  2. After every nap. Sleeping resets the bladder clock. Puppy opens eyes? You carry or walk them out immediately.
  3. After every meal. Eating activates the digestive system — most puppies need to go within 5 to 15 minutes of finishing food.
  4. After drinking water. A big drink after play or a meal means a trip out within minutes.
  5. After every play session. Excitement and movement squeeze the bladder. End play, go out — every time.
  6. After chewing a bone or toy. Ten to twenty minutes of sustained chewing usually means a pee is coming.
  7. Last thing at night. Final trip out, calm, boring — then straight to the crate or sleeping spot.

Plus the regular interval trips on the schedule below. Yes, it's a dozen or more trips a day at first. That's normal — the AKC notes you may go out a dozen times or more in a 24-hour period with a very young puppy.

The hour-by-hour schedule: weeks 8–10

Use this as your starting template. Shift the times to fit your household, but keep the structure: wake up, eat, play, nap, repeat.

  • 6:30am — Wake up: outside immediately, same spot every time. Big praise and a treat when they finish.
  • 6:45am — Breakfast in the crate or a confined area.
  • 7:00am — Outside again (post-meal trip). Then 15–20 minutes of supervised play.
  • 7:30am — Outside after play. Then crate for a morning nap — the crate helps them hold it, since dogs naturally avoid soiling their sleeping space.
  • 9:30am — Up from nap: straight outside. Then supervised free time in one puppy-proofed room.
  • 10:30am — Outside on the interval, even if they seem fine. Chew toy time.
  • 11:30am — Outside. Lunch, then outside again (post-meal). 5-minute training session, then outside once more after.
  • 12:30pm — Outside. Afternoon nap in the crate.
  • 2:30pm — Up from nap: outside. Supervised play, then outside again when play ends.
  • 3:30pm — Outside on the interval. Confined rest or chew time.
  • 4:30pm — Outside. More free time — accidents spike in late afternoon, so keep them tethered to you or in sight.
  • 5:30pm — Dinner, then outside (post-meal). Evening play session, then outside after.
  • 7:00pm — Outside after play. Calm evening from here: chews, cuddling, no wild play.
  • 8:00pm — Outside. Pick up the water bowl now so the bladder is as empty as possible before bed (they've had all day to drink — this is safe).
  • 9:30pm — Final trip outside: calm, quiet, boring. Then crate for the night.
  • ~1:30am — One middle-of-the-night trip. Carry them out, no talking, no playing, straight back to bed.

At this age, always go with them — you need to see it happen so you can reward it. An unrewarded outdoor pee teaches nothing.

Weeks 10–12: stretching the intervals

By 10 weeks, bladder capacity is growing. Keep the trigger trips the same, but stretch the between-trigger intervals from about 1 hour to 1.5 hours, then toward 2 hours by week 12. If accidents increase, you stretched too fast — drop back for a few days and try again.

  • Daytime: 1 hour → 1.5 hours → 2 hours between interval trips.
  • Nights: Most puppies this age still need one overnight trip. Push the middle-of-the-night alarm 30 minutes later each week. If the puppy whines before your alarm, you pushed too far — back up. Many puppies start sleeping through the night between 12 and 16 weeks, but there's a wide range.
  • Crate time: Never crate longer than the bladder rule allows (2–3 hours max at this age during the day). If you work away from home, arrange a midday break — a neighbor, a friend, or a dog walker. See our first-year cost breakdown for what midday help typically costs.

The crate is doing real work here: because dogs avoid soiling where they sleep, a correctly sized crate teaches bladder tolerance between trips. If your crate setup isn't right yet, read our 14-day crate training plan and the breed-by-breed crate sizing chart — crate training and potty training are really one project.

What to do outside: the 5-minute protocol

Every trip out follows the same boring ritual. Boring is the point — you're building a habit.

  1. Same spot, every time. The lingering smell tells the puppy what this location is for.
  2. Use a cue word. Pick one — "go potty," "hurry up" — and say it quietly while they're going, not before. The word attaches to the act through repetition.
  3. Wait, without rushing. Give them up to 5 minutes. If nothing happens, go back inside and try again in 15 minutes — and keep them confined or watched in between so they don't go inside during the gap.
  4. Reward after they finish — not mid-stream. Praising too early can startle a puppy into stopping halfway, and then they finish indoors. Wait until they're done, then praise and deliver the treat within 2–3 seconds.
  5. Treat like it's rent money. For the first month, every outdoor pee and poop earns a high-value treat. You can fade the treats later — but not yet.

Accidents: the no-drama protocol

The accident rule: If you catch them mid-act, interrupt calmly — a neutral "oops" or a hand clap — pick them up (or leash them), and take them outside to finish. If you find it after the fact, say nothing and clean it up. Never punish. Punishment doesn't teach a puppy where to go; it teaches them to hide it from you, which turns one accident into a stealth habit.

Cleanup is training, not housekeeping. Clean every accident with an enzymatic cleaner — it breaks down the odor proteins regular soap leaves behind, and leftover scent is what draws the puppy back to the same spot. Blot liquid thoroughly first, apply the cleaner, and let it soak for the time on the label. Our new dog essentials checklist includes the cleanup supplies to buy before day one.

Log the accidents. A quick note on your phone — time, what happened before — reveals patterns within days: always 45 minutes after the afternoon nap, always when the kids get home. Every pattern is a schedule fix waiting to happen.

The supervision system: why "watching" isn't enough

The whole schedule fails if the puppy is unsupervised indoors — every unnoticed accident is practice for the wrong behavior. The rule: eyes on, or contained.

  • Tether or follow: When the puppy is loose, you're watching. Pre-elimination signals are subtle — sudden floor sniffing, circling, wandering away from play, an abrupt stop mid-activity. Spot it and you have about 30 seconds to get outside.
  • Contained: When you can't actively watch — cooking, on a call — crate or pen the puppy. Confinement is accident prevention, and it teaches the puppy to hold it between trips.
  • Expand freedom gradually: one puppy-proofed room at a time. Rushing this is the #1 cause of "relapses" at 4–5 months.

Should you use puppy pads?

Probably not, if you can avoid it. The AKC's position is worth hearing: pads teach the puppy that going indoors is sometimes acceptable, which means you're training two conflicting rules at once — and you'll have to untrain one of them later.

There are legitimate exceptions: high-rise living where the elevator trip takes 10 minutes, extreme winter weather for very small breeds, or a work schedule where no one can get home midday. If you must use pads, place them in one fixed spot near the door and inch them toward the door each week. It's a bridge, not a destination.

When to worry (and when to just wait)

Normal: accidents most days at 8–10 weeks; excitement peeing (a little squirt when greeting people) through about 6 months; a bad week after a good week — regression is part of the process, not proof of failure.

Talk to your vet if: the puppy is straining to pee, peeing in tiny dribbles many times an hour with urgency, you see blood in the urine, or a steadily improving puppy suddenly regresses hard with no change in routine. Frequent urgent urination can signal a urinary tract infection, especially in female puppies — and no training plan fixes a medical problem. As always, we're not your vet: when something looks off, call yours.

FAQ

How long does potty training take?
Most puppies are reliably house-trained somewhere between 4 and 6 months old, though some take up to a year. Small breeds often take longer — not because they're harder to train, but because their bladders are physically smaller.
Can I punish my puppy for accidents?
No — including "rubbing their nose in it." Punishment can't teach a puppy what they should have done, and even harsh interrupting teaches them to hide accidents instead. Prevent, redirect, reward — that's the whole method.
Why does my puppy pee right after coming back inside?
Almost always one of three reasons: they didn't actually finish outside (you praised mid-stream and startled them), they were distracted and never went, or too much time passed since the last trip. Fix: go back out immediately, stay boring, give them 5 minutes, and reward the moment they finish.
Should I wake my puppy to go out at night?
At 8–10 weeks, yes — set one alarm for a calm, silent trip out. A puppy that never gets the chance to fail builds the habit faster. By 12–16 weeks, drop the alarm; most will wake you, because the crate has taught them to hold it and signal.
