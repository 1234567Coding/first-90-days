What Size Crate for My Puppy? A Breed-by-Breed Sizing Chart

By The First 90 Days Team · Published September 21, 2026 · 8 min read

This article contains affiliate links. If you buy through them, we may earn a commission at no extra cost to you. Read our full disclosure.

Buy the crate too small and your puppy can't stand up. Buy it too big and they'll designate one end as a bathroom. Here's the chart that settles it — 40+ popular breeds with recommended crate dimensions, plus the divider-panel trick that means you only ever buy one crate. (And once the right crate arrives, follow our 14-day crate training plan — sizing and training are one project.)

The golden rule: stand, turn, lie down — plus 2–4 inches

The right crate lets your dog stand up without crouching, turn around comfortably, and lie down stretched out — with 2 to 4 inches of clearance beyond that. That's it. That's the whole rule, and it applies whether you have a Chihuahua or a Great Dane.

Why the precision matters: a crate's training power comes from a dog's instinct not to soil their sleeping area. Give a puppy a crate sized for a dog twice their size and the instinct breaks — there's room for a bedroom and a bathroom. Too small is just as bad: a dog that can't stretch out won't settle, and some dogs will hold their bladder dangerously long rather than soil a cramped space.

Why puppies need a divider panel (buy for adult size, partition for now)

Here's the move that saves you buying three crates: buy the crate your puppy will need as an adult, and use the divider panel to shrink the living space now. Most wire crates include a divider or sell one cheaply as an add-on. Slide it in so the puppy's section follows the golden rule, then move it back every few weeks as they grow.

Without a divider, you'd buy a small crate at 8 weeks, a medium at 16 weeks, and a large at 6 months — roughly $150–$200 in redundant crates. With a divider, you buy once: $60–$120 for most breeds.

When in doubt, size up one. A slightly roomy crate is a minor inefficiency; a cramped one is a welfare problem. The divider handles the "too big for now" case automatically.

Measuring your specific dog in 60 seconds

Charts are estimates — breed lines vary. For a perfect fit, take three measurements while the dog is standing:

  1. Length: tip of nose to base of tail. Add 4 inches.
  2. Height: floor to top of head (ears up if they're prick-eared). Add 4 inches.
  3. Width: rarely the constraint, but check the dog can turn around — most crates are plenty wide if length and height fit.

For a growing puppy, measure against the breed's expected adult numbers (ask your breeder or vet, or use the chart above) and buy that size with a divider.

Wire vs. plastic vs. soft-sided: which type for which situation

  • Wire crates — the default for most owners. Great airflow, fold flat, usually include a divider, and the dog can see out (less isolation). Downsides: not escape-proof for determined chewers, and some dogs feel exposed — fix with a crate cover or blanket.
  • Plastic kennels — best for travel (most are airline-approved) and for anxious dogs that prefer a darker den. Downsides: no divider option, harder to clean thoroughly, and poor visibility makes training check-ins harder.
  • Soft-sided crates — lightweight and portable, great as a second crate for travel or the car once the dog is already crate-trained. Not for puppies or chewers — one determined night and there's a hole in the mesh.

Our recommendation for first-time owners: one wire crate with a divider for home. Add a soft-sided one later for travel if you need it. For the rest of the day-one shopping list — crate mats, covers, and everything else — see our new dog essentials checklist.

When to size up: signs the crate is too small

  • The dog can't stand fully upright without the head touching the top
  • They sleep curled in a ball because stretching out doesn't fit
  • They hesitate or have to fold themselves to turn around
  • Growing puppies: reassess the divider position every 2–3 weeks — if the puppy fills the section nose-to-tail, move the divider back

FAQ

Can the crate be too big?
Yes — for a puppy in training. Excess space breaks the don't-soil-the-den instinct, and puppies will use the far corner as a bathroom. The fix isn't a smaller crate; it's a divider panel in a correctly-sized adult crate. For an already house-trained adult dog, a roomy crate is perfectly fine. Getting the size right is also one of the biggest levers in potty training — see our hour-by-hour potty training schedule.
One crate or two (bedroom + living room)?
Start with one. Hauling one wire crate between rooms for two weeks is mildly annoying; buying two crates before you know your dog's habits is expensive. The exception: a two-story house where carrying a 42-inch crate up and down stairs daily will make you hate crate training. In that case, a second basic crate upstairs is worth it.
