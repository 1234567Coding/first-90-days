Crate Training a Puppy: The 14-Day Method That Actually Works

By The First 90 Days Team · Published September 24, 2026 · 9 min read

It's night two. The puppy is screaming in the crate, your neighbors are awake, and you're Googling "is crate training cruel" at 2am. It's not cruel, and night two is the worst night. What you need isn't reassurance — it's a plan with exact timings. Here it is: 14 days, day by day.

Why crate training works (and when it doesn't)

Dogs are den animals. A properly introduced crate taps into that instinct: a small, enclosed space where they sleep and feel safe. It also does three practical things for you: it accelerates potty training (dogs avoid soiling their sleeping area), it keeps a chewing puppy from destroying your home or eating something dangerous, and it gives the dog a place that is theirs when the house gets chaotic.

When doesn't it work? When the crate is used as punishment, when the puppy is left in it far longer than their bladder allows, or when it's introduced too fast — day one, door slammed, lights out. That creates a dog that panics at the sight of the crate, and it's the most common way first-time owners break crate training. This 14-day plan exists specifically to avoid that.

Before day 1: picking the right crate and where to put it

You need exactly one crate to start, and it should be sized for the dog's adult size with a divider panel — not sized for the puppy. A crate that's the right size for an 8-week-old is useless by week 12. The divider lets you shrink the living space as the puppy grows (see our breed-by-breed crate sizing chart for your breed's adult dimensions). The golden rule: enough room to stand up, turn around, and lie down — plus 2 to 4 inches. Any bigger and the puppy will designate one end as a bathroom.

For most first-time owners, a wire crate with a divider is the right call: good airflow, you can see in, and the puppy can see out (less isolation panic). A crate cover helps with nighttime crying — our new dog essentials checklist covers which crate add-ons are worth buying and which to skip. Put the crate in your bedroom for the first two weeks — proximity at night dramatically reduces crying, and you'll hear a genuine potty whimper before it becomes a full accident.

Days 1–3: door open, meals in the crate, 5-minute closed sessions

Leave the crate set up with the door fixed open. Your only job these three days is making the crate the best place in the house:

  • Feed every meal inside the crate, bowl placed at the back, door open. The puppy should walk in voluntarily within a day or two.
  • Toss 5–10 treats into the crate at random times during the day. Don't lure or point — let the puppy "discover" them.
  • Day 2: first closed-door sessions. Close the door for 5 minutes while you're sitting right next to it, treat through the bars, open the door. Repeat 3–4 times a day.
  • Day 3: extend to 10 minutes, and start stepping out of the room for 1–2 minutes while the door is closed.

At night, keep the crate next to your bed. Expect crying on nights 1–3. Potty the puppy right before bed, then settle them in with a chew. See the crying protocol below.

Days 4–7: building duration while you're home

Now you build the two separate skills: being in the crate, and being in the crate without you.

  • Day 4–5: 15-minute closed sessions, 4–5 times a day. Leave the room for half the session. Feed one meal a day with the door closed.
  • Day 6: First 30-minute session while you're home but in another room. Give a stuffed Kong or long-lasting chew at the start — the crate should predict good things.
  • Day 7: Two 30-minute sessions plus the overnight. By now, most puppies walk into the crate on their own at bedtime if the routine is consistent.

Still crying at night? Normal — it typically resolves by night 5–7 if you're consistent. What you must not do: let a crying puppy out and then put them straight back in. That's a lottery ticket — the puppy learns crying sometimes works, which makes it ten times harder to extinguish.

Days 8–14: nighttime routine, full nights, the first workday test

  • Days 8–10: Lock in the bedtime routine: final potty, crate, chew, lights out, same time every night. Move the crate a few feet farther from your bed each night if you plan to relocate it eventually — don't move it across the house in one jump.
  • Days 11–12: Most puppies now sleep 5–6 hours straight. If yours does, stop setting a middle-of-the-night alarm and only respond to genuine wake-ups.
  • Days 13–14: The first workday test. Before you need it for real, do a trial: crate the puppy for 2 hours (the max for an 8–10 week old) while you leave the house. A pet camera helps — you want to see settling within 10–15 minutes, not escalating panic.

By day 14, success looks like this: the puppy goes in voluntarily at bedtime, sleeps through most of the night, and settles within 10 minutes during daytime sessions. That's a crate-trained puppy. It won't be perfect — adolescence will test it — but the foundation is set.

The crying protocol: what to do at 2am (and what not to do)

The 2am rule: Wait 5 minutes. If the crying is escalating and frantic, go check — calmly, in the dark, no talking, no eye contact. Leash, outside, potty, straight back in the crate. If the crying is rhythmic and complaining (not panicked), give it 10 minutes before intervening. A puppy that learns 2am means a party will throw that party every night.

Do: make sure they pottied right before bed; keep nighttime boring; cover the crate with a blanket (leaving airflow) to reduce stimulation; run a white-noise machine or fan.

Don't: yell at the crate; let them out to "calm down" and then re-crate (rewards the crying); move them to your bed as a solution (that's fine as a choice, but don't pretend it's crate training).

Red flags that aren't normal: drooling, bent crate bars, bloody paws, or full panic that doesn't fade after a week — that's beyond adjustment crying. Talk to your vet and consider a certified trainer; some dogs need a different confinement strategy.

3 mistakes that ruin crate training

  1. Too much, too fast. Day-one full nights in the crate with the door shut is how you manufacture a crate phobia. Every day you skip the gradual build costs you a week of retraining later.
  2. The crate as punishment. The second you shove an angry "go to your crate!" at a misbehaving puppy, the crate stops being a den and becomes jail. Time-outs happen in a boring bathroom, never the crate.
  3. An inconsistent schedule. Dogs learn patterns, not lectures. Random crate times teach the puppy that the crate is unpredictable, which is the root of most crate anxiety. Same order of events every day matters more than same clock time.

FAQ

How long can a puppy stay in the crate?
The rule of thumb is one hour per month of age, up to about 6 hours — so 2 hours for an 8-week-old, 3 hours at 12 weeks. Adult dogs can do 4–6 hours comfortably. Longer than a workday with no break is too long for any dog, crated or not.
My puppy poops in the crate — why?
Almost always one of three things: the crate is too big (no divider, or divider set wrong — excess space becomes the bathroom), the puppy was left in longer than they can hold it, or they're anxious. Fix the size first, then the schedule. Never punish — it teaches them to hide it, not hold it.
